# Reference voice

The voice for anything a reader consults rather than reads through: book chapters that define a
mechanic, index and catalogue copy, and the entries a search result lands on.

Open with a direct identification of the subject.

The first paragraph should answer the most likely factual question without preamble.

Use neutral descriptive language for reference sections.

Separate fact from interpretation.

Avoid second-person address unless the section gives the player or the facilitator instructions.
Rule text is the exception: it is written in the second person throughout, because it tells the
reader what they do.

Marketing diction is refused by the check, in both languages. What survives here is the rule a check
cannot express: a claim about an entry stays proportional to what the entry actually does.

Prefer short semantic sections over long essay flow. A heading names what is under it.

## Entry points

Most readers arrive at an entry, not at a chapter. An entry has to stand alone: it cannot assume the
reader has read the chapter that defines its terms.

That does not mean restating the chapter. It means naming the mechanic precisely enough that the
keyword links carry the reader to the definition. This is the practical reason keyword spelling
matters: it is the codex's cross referencing, and it only works when the spelling is canonical.

Define an uncommon setting term before leaning on it. Do not define a term the vocabularies already
own; link it instead.

## The opening carries the page

Whatever quotes the page quotes the beginning of it. A search result, a tooltip and the page's own
meta description are all built from the opening, so the first sentence has to answer the likely
question without depending on anything after it.

For a skill that is literal: the first 160 characters of the description become the page's meta
description, cut at a word boundary. `site-copy.md` has the mechanics. Write the opening so that
the cut lands after the meaning, not before it.

## Shapes that survive being lifted

A definition, a short list where membership matters, and a table where the facts are genuinely
tabular all keep their sense when pulled out of the page. A paragraph describing three criteria does
not; the table of the three does.

Use them where the content is that shape and nowhere else. A table of one column is a list, a list
of prose sentences is a paragraph wearing bullets, and a heading phrased as a question the page does
not answer underneath it is worse than no heading.

Do not manufacture a question and answer section, a summary block, or structured markup that the
visible text does not support.
