Scothans are children of two peoples. A Fir Bolg's antlers rising above an elven brow; a troll's tusks sit beneath a human nose. Scales, tails, voices and old bloodlines turn up in families that have no use for them. Children soon learn which features draw a curious glance and which make their neighbour bolt the door.

*Scothan* is an old Galwarian word for *mixed blood*, *mongrel* or *bastard*, depending on who says it. Scholars favour the first translation, drunks the last. For centuries the name was imposed on people. Now Scothans use it among themselves.

> **Tiar:** "Before the matrons, you will cover those Fir Bolg antlers. They are hideous."
>
> **Shadeheart:** "My mother never asked me to cover them, Your Majesty."
>
> **Tiar:** "Your mother never asked anything of you. It shows. Cover them, or have them cut."
>
> **Shadeheart:** "Cutting them off will not make me an elf."
>

## A bad omen

![](../../media/art/placeholder-16_9-og.png)

Before the Second Radiance, a Scothan birth could empty a room. In some elven kingdoms, midwives refused to attend it. An orc clan might raise the child and still deny them inheritance. In a halfling village, a clerk could leave the parents unnamed in the register. When the harvest failed, everyone knew where to point.

Parents hid children, left them at temples or sent them to distant kin. Those who grew up found work where strangers were needed: translating at a toll gate, driving a caravan through disputed country, guiding soldiers over an unfamiliar pass. Each job brought them to a new town, with a new clerk to ask who their parents were.

## Allies of humankind

Humans arrived on Eden with weapons and ambitions of their own. The old omens meant nothing to them. A child with an orcish jaw and elven eyes drew a stare, and then a ruler asked whether they knew the road through the mountains. Scothans knew which border lords demanded tolls and how to greet a stranger at an elven gate. Human courts hired them to carry messages and lead parties through Eden. When human armies crossed the elven borders, many Scothans marched with them through more than half a century of war. The pay drew some; others wanted citizenship or remembered the gates once shut against their mothers. The fighting in Brécelann has left accounts in both camps. Its elven houses still ask a Scothan visitor which army their family served.

## A new generation

The wars wore down their armies and treasuries. Borders settled and markets reopened. Soldiers who had faced one another across a palisade began haggling over grain in the same square. Their children played among the stalls while the adults disputed weights and measures. In these towns, a Scothan child might have neighbours of the same parentage and a grandmother who knew their human friends by name. Clerks learned to write *half-elf* and *half-orc* in the birth register. A child of less familiar parentage still faced the empty line. A clerk could send the family away to find a witness, then leave the book open on his desk until closing time.

## Between two peoples

![](../../media/art/placeholder-16_9-og.png)

A half-elf may dine at a noble table while the guards search a half-goblin at the servants' entrance. A Scothan swordmaster can win a tournament and have to show their papers at the bridge on the way home. Familiar parentage helps in some places. In others, the watch knows the family name and stops them anyway.

Some Scothans learn to pass. They file down horns, bind tails beneath coats or shorten a family name. Figaro, born of gnome and tiefling blood, hid his horns for years. His acquaintances took him for a bad-tempered gnome. A Scothan who lets their horns show may be praised in the guildhall and searched at the next gate. A mother who has seen the watch at her door may beg her child to put the hat back on.

## The pride of the Faebies

Shadeheart, daughter of Princess Lyra and granddaughter of Queen Tiar Vyrefiel, refused to cover her Fir Bolg antlers before the matrons. Her answer to the queen travelled through the court by supper. Soon afterward she founded the Faebies. Their halls give Scothans work and a place to sleep when the watch comes looking. The doorways are wide enough for antlers. Nobody asks a newcomer to shorten their name.

The guild has interrupted auctions, forged travel papers and freed prisoners. It has publicly named officials who kept separate rules for Scothans. Members argue about these methods. A raid can get a captive home and bring the watch to every Faebie door the next morning. Magistrates have warrants for several of them; the families sheltering those members keep the papers out of sight.

## The Garthae

The Galwarian word *Garthae* means something between *impossible* and *forbidden*. It names Scothans whose parents, by every known rule, could not have conceived them. A midwife can name the mother and still have no account of the conception that satisfies the village. Some families speak of a god's intervention. Others admit to alchemy. Their neighbours usually settle on sorcery. A child of one of Charon's Nnor is always called Garthae, since the Nnor reproduce by self-fertilisation. A child born partly of a Cindersöhls bears the name as well, though that artificial body was never carried in a womb. There are other cases, each with a story that will be repeated long after the family has left town. A neighbour who wants the Weitzguard's favour need only tell an Oracle where the child went.

## Hunted by the Oracles

The Weitzguard sends its Oracles to find Garthae and investigate their origins. Most investigations end with an order to kill. Official doctrine treats each child as evidence of a breach in Eden's natural order. An impossible bloodline could be made again, the Oracles argue, and whoever learned the method might put it to use in war. They search homes for ritual objects and question anyone who attended the birth. Their work has filled unmarked graves.

Garthae travel under borrowed names and learn which inns ask for birth records. Faebies sometimes hide them. Hedge-witches sell charms meant to muddle divination, though few sellers will offer a refund if an Oracle finds the buyer. Families burn registers and tell the Oracles that the child died years ago. When an Oracle returns to test that story, the child may already be grown and working in the next town.
